Aira

A gentle habit companion for women with PCOS, built around energy, not willpower.

My roleUI lead, plus user research and interviews
TeamGopichand, Simran, Niranjana and me
Timeline6 weeks
ToolsFigma

Too many apps, and none of them talk to each other

PCOS affects around 1 in 5 women of reproductive age. It touches sleep, insulin, mood and energy, not just periods. Yet most people manage it with a stack of separate apps: one for periods, one for food, one for sleep.

So a bad night's sleep gets logged in one app, and the next day's sugar cravings in another. Nobody connects the two. Logging everything by hand becomes a chore, people burn out on tracking, and they're left with more guilt than answers.

The question we started with: why do people with PCOS struggle to keep up healthy habits, and what support would actually help them stay consistent?

Research

5In-depth interviews, ages 22 to 35
25Survey responses, 14 with PCOS
1Interview with an endocrinologist
+Desk research on apps and PCOS care

What the survey showed

  • 12/14Symptoms don't follow a schedule. Their worst time of day "varies too much to tell", so fixed routines break easily.
  • 9/14Low energy is the biggest blocker when symptoms flare up.
  • 9/14Starting isn't the problem. Staying is. They start strong but can't stay consistent.
  • 10/14They want support that fits them. Personalized habit suggestions were the most wanted kind of help.

What the interviews added

  • Symptoms feel like personal failure. One person told us: "I feel like I'm fighting my own body every single day."
  • They want to know their capacity. The real morning question: do I have the energy to push today, or do I need to rest?
  • Knowing why helps. "You feel anxious because of where you are in your cycle" brought relief.
  • Tone matters. Judgmental notifications made people anxious, not active.

Melanie, 28, the overwhelmed striver

She starts every week with big plans and crashes by Wednesday. She feels tired but wired. She doesn't need an app that tells her to do more. She needs one that tells her when it's okay to do less.

Melanie is built from our five interviews, and from the start-strong, crash-by-midweek pattern that 9 of 14 survey respondents described.

help people with PCOS build habits that adapt to how much energy they have today, instead of failing them on the hard days?

One daily read on your energy

Aira brings five parts of PCOS health together: sleep, nutrition, movement, stress and cycle. It turns them into one score. On a low day it asks for less. On a good day it helps you make the most of it.

  1. CheckSee your Hormonal Readiness Score, from 0 to 100.
  2. CalibrateSee when your energy is likely to dip, based on your sleep and cycle phase.
  3. ActPick food and movement that match your score, like yoga instead of HIIT on a low day.

How we modelled the score

Readiness =Sleep capacity−Insulin load−Cortisol load+Restorative bonus

Sleep debt lowers your starting point. Sugary meals cost more after a short night. A hard workout on a stressful day costs more too. Walking or yoga earns points back. Users never see the maths, just one number shown like a battery.

The Energy tab

This is the heart of the app, and I designed every screen in it. The day starts with the readiness score. Then Aira asks: how is this showing up for you? Instead of a form, you pick from soft emotion shapes, so the check-in feels calm, not clinical.

From there you can talk it through by voice, or drop into Zen Mode for a short guided breathing break. These clips are recorded from the Figma prototype.

Sleep and Wind-down

Sleep was one of the hardest habits in our survey, so I gave it its own space. It explains what last night means for today in plain language, linked to your cycle phase.

The Wind-down window breaks the evening into steps, three hours, two hours and one hour before bed, with one-minute reads on why each habit helps.

Learn and Track

The Learn tab gives short, trustworthy reads that match what you're going through that day. I also helped design Cycle Insights and Activity Insights in the Track tab, which suggest gentle movement based on your cycle phase.

A home screen widget and warm notifications keep Aira present without nagging, like "Nice work getting some movement in, your hormones love steady routines."

Design decisions

Dark mode first

Many people with PCOS get migraines and are sensitive to light, so the app is dark by default.

No alarm colours for low scores

A low readiness score shows in calm colours, not alarm red, so a hard day never feels like a failure.

No calorie counts

Meals are logged with simple tags, and the numbers stay hidden.

Less typing

Sleep and steps come in from Apple Health, so people don't log everything by hand.

Testing and iteration

We tested the wireframes by asking people to complete onboarding, check their score, use the widget, review their energy patterns and react to notifications.

We tested with our whole class, which included five or six women, and with some of the people we interviewed.

  • 01Complex hormone graphs became one battery-style score.
  • 02We added passive data from Apple Health to cut manual entry.
  • 03Journaling became a one-tap "Wired or Tired?" widget.
  • 04Notifications moved from reminders to celebrating small wins.

One shared system

We built a shared design system so four people could design different tabs while the app still felt like one product. We chose the colours together as a team, and I designed the components: cards, buttons, emotion shapes and navigation.

What's next

Aira is a concept. The next step would be testing it with real users over a few weeks. Features we'd explore:

  • Glucose monitor sync for real-time insulin insight
  • Crash forecasts that warn of energy dips a day ahead
  • Anonymous community tips from others in the same cycle phase
  • A view for doctors to see patterns between appointments

What I learned

I went in thinking people with PCOS needed more tracking. The interviews showed the opposite: they needed less guilt. Tone turned out to be a design decision as big as any screen, down to a low score never showing up in red. Next, I'd test whether the readiness score actually changes what people do on a low day.
